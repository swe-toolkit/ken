# Count the population before you place it inside a mechanism

I read a two-stage lifecycle carefully — Stage 1 `ContinuationFrameRequirement`,
Stage 2 `ContinuationFrameIdentity`, and a publication gate whose refusal reads
*"a continuation input has no finalized availability, so its generated frame
requirement was never resolved to an exact context identity."* From that I ruled
that five governed rows were **blocked at that gate**, and named the lawful
successor as *finish the lifecycle*. The Steward framed and kicked a node on it.

**The probe refuted it.** Stage 2 was already correctly sequenced and running,
and all five rows carried **zero** projections and **zero** frame requirements.
They never enter that machinery. There was nothing to finalize; the node's
premise was empty, and the real boundary was the larger one (a planner relation
or transported operand) that my ruling had told the Steward was *not* needed.

**The error is a category slip, not a misreading.** Everything I read about the
mechanism was true. What I never established was **where the population sits
inside it**. A gate's refusal message describes records that *reach* the gate —
it is silent about a population that produces no record at all. Reading the
mechanism tells you what happens to things that arrive; only a count tells you
whether anything arrives.

**Why it is seductive.** A well-documented mechanism invites you to locate a
stuck population by matching symptoms to its stages. The match is usually
plausible, and a plausible placement produces a *smaller, more attractive*
successor than the truth — which is exactly why it gets adopted.

**How to apply.**

- **Before naming a successor, count what the population produces at the seam
  the successor targets.** Zero is a completely different node from "some, but
  unfinalized", and the two are indistinguishable from the mechanism's prose.
- **A refusal message is evidence about arrivals, never about absences.** Same
  family as [[never-reached-is-two-different-facts-and-only-instrumenting-every-route-tells-them-apart]]
  — declined-at-a-gate and never-reached-the-gate look identical downstream.
- **Apply your own counting demands to your own rulings.** I required a count
  before anyone could call an empty key set a finding, and in the same day
  asserted a lifecycle position with no count. The discipline you impose as a
  reviewer is the one you are least likely to impose on your own design ruling,
  because a ruling feels like analysis rather than a claim.
- **It applies to a FOLLOW-UP'S SIZE, not only a successor's target.** Second
  instance, same day: reviewing a mapping pin I wrote that the missing symmetric
  row was *"same file, same shape, a few lines"* — having checked nothing about
  existing coverage. The Steward found one of the two forms was already asserted
  elsewhere, so the honest answer was possibly **one row, not two**. Sizing a
  follow-up is a claim about a population (what is already covered) and needs the
  same count. Both times I required a count from someone else in the same breath.
- **A "do X or narrow the claim" alternative is not always a real either/or.**
  In the same review I offered "add the row, or narrow the header." Narrowing
  would have left half the mapping unasserted **in a node whose stated premise
  was that the mapping is asserted nowhere** — the node undermining itself, not
  a lesser version of the fix. Check whether the weaker option contradicts the
  node's own reason for existing before offering it as equivalent.
- **When a successor turns out empty, withdraw the successor — do not repair the
  node.** Folding a correction into a node whose premise has been falsified
  hides the falsification; the scope owner needs to re-frame from the corrected
  boundary.
